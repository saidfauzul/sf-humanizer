# English pattern catalog

Patterns run from strongest to weakest. A **strong** pattern can be acted on alone. Act on a **weak** pattern only when it appears with another pattern in the same sentence or paragraph. Check the "Do not flag" section before changing anything.

## EN-01 Stock AI vocabulary (strong)

- Look for: delve, leverage, robust, seamless, foster, navigate (figurative), landscape, underscore, pivotal.
- Why: these words appear far more often in generated text than in ordinary work writing.
- Fix: use the plain word that says what happened, or cut it.
- Before: We will leverage a robust framework to foster seamless vendor onboarding.
- After: We will use this framework to make vendor onboarding smoother.

## EN-02 "It is important to note" (strong)

- Look for: "it is important to note", "it is worth noting", "it should be noted that".
- Why: the phrase announces content without adding any.
- Fix: delete it and state the point. If the point needs weight, move it to the start of the paragraph.
- Before: It is important to note that the Bid Bond is valid for 60 days.
- After: The Bid Bond is valid for 60 days.

## EN-03 Negative parallelism (strong)

- Look for: "it's not X, it's Y", "not just X but Y" used to create drama.
- Why: it knocks down a claim nobody made.
- Fix: state Y directly.
- Before: This review isn't a formality. It's a safeguard for every Contract Award.
- After: This review protects every Contract Award.

## EN-04 Generic openers and closers (strong)

- Look for: "In today's fast-paced world", "In conclusion", "I hope this email finds you well" where it adds nothing, "Together we can".
- Why: they fit any topic, so they say nothing about this one.
- Fix: begin with the point and end on the decision or next step.
- Before: In today's evolving governance landscape, oversight matters. Please submit the Statement of Work by 15 March.
- After: Please submit the Statement of Work by 15 March.

## EN-05 Summarizing closers (strong)

- Look for: a final sentence repeating the paragraph, often with "This shows", "Ultimately", "Overall".
- Why: the reader has just read it.
- Fix: if it adds no fact, fold anything needed into the sentence before. Do not lose information.
- Before: The audit found three contracts without acceptance records. This highlights gaps in contract documentation.
- After: The audit found three contracts without acceptance records, so contract files are incomplete.

## EN-06 Stacked transitions (strong)

- Look for: Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, In addition opening sentence after sentence.
- Why: logic is announced instead of carried by the order of ideas.
- Fix: remove most of them. Keep one where it marks real contrast or cause.
- Before: Moreover, vendors must report monthly. Furthermore, the Contracting Officer reviews each report.
- After: Vendors must report monthly, and the Contracting Officer reviews each report.

## EN-07 "Not only ... but also" (strong)

- Look for: the construction used for ordinary addition.
- Why: it inflates a plain "and" into a revelation.
- Fix: use "and", or two sentences.
- Before: The policy not only sets approval limits but also defines escalation.
- After: The policy sets approval limits and defines escalation.

## EN-08 Forced parallelism (strong)

- Look for: consecutive clauses with an identical shape that the content does not require.
- Why: the rhythm sounds like a slogan rather than a report.
- Fix: say it directly and let the structure differ.
- Before: Clear rules build trust. Clear roles build speed. Clear records build confidence.
- After: Clear rules build trust, and clear roles speed up the work. Good records give people confidence.

## EN-09 Rhetorical questions (strong)

- Look for: a question the writer answers immediately in work writing.
- Why: it borrows a speech device that a document reader does not need.
- Fix: turn it into a statement.
- Before: Why does the estimate need updating? Because market prices have moved since January.
- After: The estimate needs updating because market prices have moved since January.

## EN-10 Bulletization of prose (strong)

- Look for: connected reasoning broken into bullets, often with bold labels.
- Why: cause and effect disappear, and the text reads like a slide.
- Fix: write a paragraph. Keep lists for real steps, requirements or items.
- Before: **Issue:** documents arrived late. **Impact:** the tender slipped. **Fix:** add staff.
- After: Documents arrived late and the tender slipped. The fix is to add staff.

## EN-11 Nominalization chains (strong)

- Look for: strings of -tion, -ment and -ance nouns joined by "of".
- Why: the actor vanishes and the reader must unpack the chain.
- Fix: name the actor and turn one noun into a verb. Locked terms stay whole.
- Before: The strengthening of the implementation of contract oversight is the focus of Procurement.
- After: Procurement is focusing on tighter contract oversight.

## EN-12 Light verbs (strong)

- Look for: "conduct an evaluation", "provide assistance", "make a determination", "carry out a review".
- Why: an empty verb takes the place of the real one.
- Fix: use the real verb: evaluate, help, determine, review.
- Before: The committee will conduct an evaluation of the bids and make a determination.
- After: The committee will evaluate the bids and decide.

## EN-13 Triplets (weak)

- Look for: three parallel adjectives or nouns with near-identical meanings, especially repeatedly.
- Why: three feels complete, even when the third item is empty.
- Fix: keep the words that differ in meaning. Never drop an item that carries a fact.
- Before: The process must be transparent, open and clear.
- After: The process must be transparent.

## EN-14 Hedging stacks (weak)

- Look for: "may potentially", "could possibly", "somewhat relatively" in one clause.
- Why: the writer seems to be avoiding a position.
- Fix: keep one hedge at the original level of certainty. Do not turn it into a certainty.
- Before: The delay may potentially affect the acceptance date.
- After: The delay may affect the acceptance date.

## EN-15 Empty intensifiers (weak)

- Look for: very, highly, significantly, truly, comprehensive with no measure.
- Why: they stand in for a concrete detail.
- Fix: cut the word. If the text gives a figure, let the figure work. Never invent a measure.
- Before: This highly comprehensive review delivered truly significant savings of 4%.
- After: This review delivered savings of 4%.

## EN-16 Uniform sentence length (weak)

- Look for: several consecutive sentences of almost the same length and shape.
- Why: people change rhythm with the content.
- Fix: combine related sentences and split any that hold two ideas.
- Before: The panel received the bids. The panel checked the bids. The panel sent the results.
- After: After receiving and checking the bids, the panel sent the results.

## EN-17 Em-dash overuse (weak)

- Look for: more than one em dash per page used to join clauses.
- Why: it has become a visible habit of generated text.
- Fix: use a comma, colon, parentheses or a new sentence.
- Before: The tender was reissued — too few vendors had bid.
- After: The tender was reissued because too few vendors had bid.

## Do not flag

- Definitions with "means" or "is" in a definitions section.
- A single "Moreover" or "In addition" in a long report.
- Standard email greetings and sign-offs, such as "Best regards" or "Kind regards".
- Salutations and closings required by a formal letter format.
- Triplets that are real enumerations, such as three named documents or three parties.
- Lists that hold real procedure steps, requirements or attachments.
- Passive voice that is standard in regulations and contracts, or where the actor is unknown.
- Established terms of art containing nominalizations, or anything in the glossary.
- Deliberate repetition of a term to avoid a second reading.
- "Significant" with a measure, or in a defined legal or statistical sense.
- A single hedge that reflects the writer's actual certainty.
- "Robust", "landscape" or "navigate" in a literal or established technical sense.
- Segments locked under locking.md.
